# Rubric

Seven dimensions, 1–10 each. Score honestly — a 7 that gets called a 9 is a case study that never improves. The overall score is the average of the two **lowest** dimensions, because a reviewer's impression is set by the weakest argument, not the strongest sentence.

Anchors are written for 3, 6, and 9 so the gaps are visible. Most competent case studies sit at 6–7 across the board.

---

## 1. Claim integrity

Is every claim traceable to something that actually happened?

- **3** — Invented metrics, vague impact ("improved engagement"), research described with no method behind it, a team project written in first person singular.
- **6** — Claims are true but unsourced. Numbers appear without saying where they came from. Nothing is false; nothing is verifiable either.
- **9** — Every number carries its source and its limits. Where there is no data, the copy says so and offers the qualitative evidence instead ("no analytics were in place; the trainer's own estimate was X"). Speculative outcomes are labelled as projections.

This dimension is a hard gate. If it scores below 5, say so before anything else — no amount of good writing survives a reviewer catching one invented number.

## 2. Specificity

Numbers, names, constraints — or generic design vocabulary?

- **3** — "Conducted user research", "iterated based on feedback", "improved the user experience". Could describe any project ever made.
- **6** — Some specifics, mostly about method rather than substance. "5 interviews" without what the interviews changed.
- **9** — The details are load-bearing. The reader learns something about the domain they didn't know: what a trainer actually does at 6am, how many clients fit in one person's memory, what breaks at client thirty-one. Specificity that only the person who did the work could produce.

The test: could this sentence appear in someone else's case study unchanged? If yes, it's not specific.

## 3. Decision visibility

Are rejected alternatives and trade-offs on the page?

- **3** — Decisions presented as inevitable. The design appears fully formed. No alternative is ever mentioned.
- **6** — Decisions are explained but only forwards — here's why this works. The obvious rival option is never named, so the reader can't tell whether it was considered or missed.
- **9** — The efficient-looking alternative is named, its appeal is granted honestly, and the reason for going the other way is a real reason rather than a preference. The cost of the chosen path is stated and accepted out loud.

Granting the rival option its due is the strongest single move available in a case study. A designer who can argue the other side and still choose reads as senior.

## 4. Plain language

Could a smart twelve-year-old follow it without re-reading?

- **3** — Dense with jargon. Abstract nouns doing the work of verbs. Sentences that need a second pass.
- **6** — Readable by designers. Would lose a founder, a recruiter, or an engineer somewhere in the middle.
- **9** — Anyone can follow it. Domain terms are used but introduced. Sentences are short. The hardest idea in the case study is explained with the smallest words available.

Not the same as dumbing down. Plain language makes complex thinking *more* visible, because there's nothing between the reader and the idea.

## 5. Human voice

Does it sound like a person who was there?

- **3** — Template energy. Framework labels used as headings. Words nobody says out loud.
- **6** — Competent professional writing with no fingerprints. Nothing wrong, nothing memorable.
- **9** — Specific rhythm, real observations, occasional plainness where a template would inflate. Reads like it was written once, by someone with an opinion, rather than assembled.

See `copy-craft.md` for the specific tells.

## 6. Evidence pairing

Does each claim have the artifact that proves it, next to it?

- **3** — Wall of text with a hero shot, or a gallery of final screens with captions.
- **6** — Artifacts are present and relevant but sit apart from the claims they support. The reader has to do the connecting.
- **9** — Each claim has its proof adjacent and legible. Rejected versions appear beside chosen ones. Process artifacts are shown at the moment they mattered, not collected in a "process" dump.

## 7. Scannability

Do the titles alone tell the story?

- **3** — Titles are section labels ("Research", "Ideation", "Wireframes"). Reading only the titles tells you nothing about the project.
- **6** — Titles name the topic of each slide. Reading them gives you a table of contents.
- **9** — Titles carry the point ("Home stays, and logging costs one extra tap"). Reading only the titles gives you the argument. First line of each body is the answer, not the wind-up.

The test: strip everything but the titles. Does a reader still understand what happened and why? That's the 15-second read, and most of them never get past it.
