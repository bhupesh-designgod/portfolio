# Structure

The eleven-section shape for a product design case study. Use it as a checklist for gaps, not as visible headings — see the note on framework labels in `copy-craft.md`.

Order matters less than coverage. A case study can rearrange these freely; what it can't do is skip one silently, because reviewers notice absences faster than they notice weak sections.

| # | Section | What it's for | Reads as a gap if missing? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title / opening | What this is, in one line a reader can repeat | Yes |
| 2 | Product overview & context | What the product is, who it's for, the situation before | Yes |
| 3 | Role & outcome | What you specifically did, with whom, over how long | Yes — always |
| 4 | Problem space — business | Why the business needed this, where it broke | Yes |
| 5 | Problem space — user | What the person on the other side was experiencing | Yes |
| 6 | Strategy & vision | What you chose to solve, and what you deliberately left out | Yes |
| 7 | Information architecture | How the thing is organised, and why that shape | Sometimes — depends on product complexity |
| 8 | Core user flows | The two or three journeys that matter, annotated | Yes |
| 9 | Module / feature breakdown | What got built, grouped by the job it does | No — can merge into flows |
| 10 | Design system foundation | Evidence of a system, not a style | Sometimes — expected for product roles |
| 11 | Key decisions & trade-offs | The three or four hard calls, each with its rejected alternative | Yes — this is the section that carries seniority |
| 12 | Outcomes & product impact | What changed, or an honest account of what couldn't be measured | Yes |
| 13 | Reflections & what's next | What you'd do differently, specifically | Yes |

## Where cases commonly go wrong structurally

**Role stated too late or too vaguely.** Section 3 exists because a reviewer's first question is always "what did *you* do." Putting it after seven slides of context means they've been reading with that question unanswered.

**Problem space split unevenly.** Business and user problems are usually the same failure seen from two ends. The strongest version of this section names the thing that connects them before splitting into the two views.

**Strategy that only says what was built.** The persuasive half is what was cut and why. A strategy section with no exclusions isn't a strategy.

**Key decisions collapsed into one slide.** Each real decision deserves its own space with the rejected option beside it. Three decisions at one slide each beats eight decisions in a bulleted list.

**Outcomes that overclaim.** If the product didn't ship, or shipped without instrumentation, say so and give what you have: the client's response, what changed in how they work, what you'd instrument first. Honesty here reads as senior. Invented numbers read as a candidate who'll invent them again.

## Multi-surface projects

When one project has two surfaces (a client app and an admin dashboard, say), running them as two linked case studies usually beats one long one. Each gets its own problem framing and its own decisions; the second can open by referring back rather than re-establishing context. If they're kept together, the joint case study needs an explicit slide on how the two surfaces relate — otherwise it reads as two projects stapled together.

## Length

Three to five case studies in a portfolio, no more. Per case study, aim for something a reviewer can get through in two to three minutes and a reader can go deep on if they want. Cut the third-best project rather than shortening the best one.
