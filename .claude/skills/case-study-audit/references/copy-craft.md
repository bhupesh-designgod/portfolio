# Copy craft

How to make case study copy read like a person wrote it once, on purpose.

## The plain-language test

Before accepting any rewrite, run it:

1. **Read it aloud.** If you run out of breath or stumble, the sentence is too long or too nested.
2. **Twelve-year-old check.** Would a bright twelve-year-old follow it? Not "would they care" — would they *follow* it. Every abstract noun is a place they'd stop.
3. **Verb check.** Are the verbs doing the work, or are they all "is", "was", "provides", "enables", "facilitates"? Weak verbs mean the real action got buried in a noun.
4. **Delete test.** Cut the first three words of the sentence. Still works? They were throat-clearing.

Plain does not mean short and choppy. It means each sentence carries one idea, in the order a person would think it.

## Words and constructions to strip

**Kill on sight:**

aspiring · passionate about · user-centric · delightful · seamless · concept project · leverage · robust · elevate · empower · streamline · holistic · intuitive (as a claim about your own work) · dive deep · unlock · game-changer · at scale (when nothing is at scale) · thoughtfully crafted · pixel-perfect

**Constructions that read as machine-written:**

- **"Not X, but Y."** One is a rhetorical choice. Three in a document is a tell.
- **Rule of three.** "Faster, clearer, and more accessible." Real observations rarely arrive in tidy triples. Use two, or four, or one.
- **Em-dash asides stacked up.** One per paragraph at most.
- **Colon-then-reveal.** "The result: a cleaner flow." Just say it.
- **Superficial -ing analysis.** "...allowing users to complete tasks more efficiently" tacked onto the end of a sentence to imply significance that wasn't demonstrated.
- **Scare quotes around invented labels.** If the concept needs quotes to exist, it isn't a concept yet.
- **Vague attribution.** "Research showed", "users felt", "it was found that". Who, how many, how did you find out?
- **Inflated symbolism.** Calling a nav bar decision "a philosophy" or a colour choice "a statement of intent".
- **Framework labels as headings.** "C-R-O-S", "HMW", "Double Diamond" as visible structure. Frameworks are scaffolding for writing — take the scaffolding down before showing the building. A reviewer who sees a methodology diagram used as the case study structure reads it as someone who confused a framework with evidence of thinking.

## Rewrite patterns

### The generic claim → the specific observation

Weak: *"Research revealed that users found the process frustrating."*

The fix isn't better adjectives, it's the actual thing observed. Ask: what did you literally see or hear? Then write that.

Strong shape: *"Four of the five people I spoke to had a screenshot of their meal plan saved to their phone gallery, because scrolling back through eight weeks of chat to find it took longer than they'd wait."*

### The inevitable decision → the argued decision

Weak: *"I decided to use a bottom navigation bar for easy access to key features."*

Strong shape names the rival, grants it, then chooses:

> Logging is what the client does most, which made logging-as-home the efficient answer. But an app that opens on a food diary is a food diary — and this product's value is a coach paying attention.

Then state what it cost: *"One extra tap to log, every day, forever. Accepted, because..."*

### The abstract problem → the concrete failure

Weak: *"The existing workflow was inefficient and did not scale."*

Strong shape puts a number or a breaking point on it: *"The trainer held roughly thirty clients in his head. That worked, and it was also the ceiling. A thirty-first client didn't cost a little more time — it cost recall he didn't have."*

### The process narration → the turning point

Nobody needs the full chronology. They need the moments where you learned something and changed course. Compress "I did A, then B, then C" into the one place the plan broke and what you did about it.

### The reflection that confesses → the reflection that demonstrates

Weak: *"I learned the importance of testing early. Next time I would test sooner."*

That's an admission with no evidence attached. Stronger: name the specific thing you got wrong, what it cost, and what you did differently *within the same project*. If you did nothing differently, say what you'd test first and why that one.

## Section-specific notes

**Titles.** Carry the point, not the topic. "Key Decision 3" is a label. "A day recalculates when a client eats off-plan" is a point. Aim for a title a reader can quote back.

**First line of any section.** The answer, not the wind-up. Delete "In order to understand the problem space, I began by..."

**Captions.** Every image gets one and it says what to notice, not what it is. "Trainer dashboard" is a filename. "The queue orders clients by how overdue their check-in is — nothing here requires the trainer to remember anyone" is a caption.

**Role statements.** Precise and slightly conservative. "I designed the interaction and visual design for the client app; a two-person dev team built it; the client owned scope" beats "I led end-to-end design." Overstated roles collapse under one interview question.

**Numbers.** Every number gets a source clause. "Roughly thirty, per the trainer's own count" survives scrutiny. "Thirty clients" invites "how do you know?"

## What not to touch

Leave alone anything that is: the user's own turn of phrase and working; a deliberate plain statement where a template would inflate; domain language the audience will know; a short sentence that earns its shortness. Editing personality out is the most common way a copy pass makes a case study worse. When a line is odd but alive, flag it as a question rather than smoothing it.
