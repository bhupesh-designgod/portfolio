## Overview

Ikarus Nest was a marketplace and crowdfunding platform for 3D printable files.
Two sides. 3D artists who sculpt and sell models, and 3D printing hobbyists who
buy and print them. Artists list individual models or run funded campaigns with
reward tiers and milestones. Buyers browse, purchase, and print.

Ikarus 3D was a studio building production 3D models and AR/VR assets for
brands. Nest was its move into a consumer product.

I joined the design team to work on the marketplace and creator surfaces:
information architecture, core screens, empty and edge states, and reusable
components for the design system.

## What the platform was betting on

<p class="k-hook">The differentiator was piracy protection. <em>Remove the download, remove the problem.</em></p>

A 3D model is a file. Once a buyer downloads it, the artist has no control over
what happens next. Copies spread. Creative Commons licensing exists, but nothing
enforces it past the download.

So Nest was designed around removing the download. Artists could set custom
licences capping prints per user. Files under those licences would never leave
the platform. Buyers would interact with them through a built-in cloud slicer,
and the sliced output would go straight to the printer over wifi through a
companion g-code sender.

Two things at once: protect artists from unauthorised use, and unify a printing
workflow that was otherwise stitched together from separate tools.

## How we reached people

Members of the team maintained relationships inside Reddit and Discord
communities where 3D artists and printing hobbyists actually spend time. That
access is what made the interviews possible. These are not people who answer
cold outreach. I contributed interviews and brought in participants through my
own contacts in the tabletop and D&D scene.

43 conversations. Most over Discord DM, some through Facebook and Instagram.

piracy, they were talking about their actual income.

## What the artists said

<p class="k-claim">Every artist raised piracy before we asked. Almost none of
them wanted it solved.</p>

Four positions, each one distinct.

<div class="k-duo k-duo--c2 k-wide">
  <div class="k-card">
    <div class="k-card__h"><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-wallet"/></svg></span><h4>The cost of doing business</h4></div>
    <p>A studio that had raised over $250,000 called piracy the cost of doing business, said the idea was about five years late, and pointed out that porting several hundred products to an unproven platform was a risk he had taken before and lost on.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="k-card">
    <div class="k-card__h"><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-megaphone"/></svg></span><h4>Free copies are marketing</h4></div>
    <p>Another was blunt that the people sharing files were never going to be customers, so the copies cost him nothing and reached people he could not. His view: build for the paying customer, not against the freeloader.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="k-card">
    <div class="k-card__h"><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-users"/></svg></span><h4>Nothing to be done</h4></div>
    <p>A third knew his work circulated on Telegram and illicit sites. He was annoyed by it and had concluded there was nothing to be done. His community reported thieves on his behalf.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="k-card">

## What the buyers said

The buyers closed the other half.

<div class="k-why k-wide">
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-download"/></svg></span><div><h4>They want to own the file</h4><p>Several described running a model through two or three tools before it ever reaches a slicer: mesh repair, UV tools, cutting a model into printable parts. A file you cannot take out is a file they cannot prepare.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-slice"/></svg></span><div><h4>They are loyal to their slicer</h4><p>Lychee, Chitubox, Cura, Bambu Studio, printer-specific software. One said flatly that everyone likes their own slicer and that locking people in would cut out most of the audience.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-wifi"/></svg></span><div><h4>Most of their printers had no wifi</h4><p>The direct-to-printer model assumed hardware the market did not have.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-clock"/></svg></span><div><h4>G-code ages badly</h4><p>One artist made a technical point nobody had raised internally. G-code cannot be scaled, and a g-code file dates as slicers improve. Distributing sliced output instead of source files puts an expiry date on someone's library.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-box"/></svg></span><div><h4>The files are large</h4><p>Between 30 and 300MB. Browsers break on them.</p></div></div>
</div>

## The turn

<p class="k-claim k-claim--goal">The research did not adjust the product.<br><em>It undercut its reason for existing.</em></p>

What it left behind was a clear brief. Artists had told us exactly what they
wanted and none of it was piracy protection. They wanted reach, payouts,
analytics, distribution, and a campaign builder that didn't fight them. Buyers
wanted to find the right file and trust it before paying.

Non-downloadable licences moved off the critical path and down the roadmap. The
first release refocused on campaigns, marketplace, portfolios, and community,
with marketing and outreach for artists as the headline.

## Onboarding

Two findings pull in opposite directions here. Artists were vocal about
verification friction. One said outright that being asked for government ID to
run a campaign would put him off. Another named identity and payment checks as
the specific reason Kickstarter was the harder platform. Buyers, meanwhile,
ranked reliability and being secure enough to hand over payment details in their
top requirements.

## Explore Marketplace

The most common complaint from buyers was finding the right thing. Search on
existing platforms was poor, and creators routinely renamed projects to avoid
copyright claims, which meant the things people were looking for were
deliberately not named what they were called. Artists' top request was
visibility.

One surface answers both. Browse for people who don't know what they want,
search and category filtering for people who do.

## Mature content

One artist told us that if pin-ups and tasteful nude figures weren't permitted
he wouldn't use the platform, because that was most of what he made. That's a
policy question that arrives as a design problem.

The answer was a gate, not a ban. Mature models stay listed and discoverable,
with the content itself held behind an interstitial for signed-out users and a
prompt to log in.

## Design system and handoff

This was before Figma Dev Mode, so behaviour had to be written down. The
annotations in the file specify conditional logic and interaction, not labels.

<figure class="k-cap k-wide">
  <div class="k-plate">
    <img src="/assets/projects/nest/annotations.png" alt="A model detail screen covered in black annotation callouts describing conditional behaviour: which icon appears on GLB files, what happens on clicking like, what a container accepts, and which control expands a section" loading="lazy" width="1601" height="1000" />
  </div>
  <figcaption>The handoff artefact. Every callout is a rule a developer would otherwise have to invent: which icon appears only on GLB files, what the like button does to its own state, what a comment box accepts, which control expands the section below it.</figcaption>
</figure>

<ol class="k-notes k-wide">
  <li><span><b>Tooltip on the inactive Publish button</b><span>Appears on hover, explaining what is still missing.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>Minimum three tags, maximum five</b><span>Enforced in the field, not on submit.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>The continue button activates only after the checkbox is ticked</b><span>Stated on the screen, so nobody ships it the other way round.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>This icon appears only on GLB files</b><span>A condition, not a decoration.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>Clicking here expands the section; content slides left on click</b><span>Direction and trigger, both written down.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>Asterisked labels mark mandatory fields</b><span>One rule, applied across every form in the file.</span></span></li>
</ol>

<div class="k-band k-wide">
  <span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-note"/></svg></span>
  <div><h4>Without Dev Mode, <em>anything not written down gets decided by whoever builds it.</em></h4><p>Which is fine for spacing and fatal for conditional logic.</p></div>
</div>

## What I'd do differently

<div class="k-why k-wide">
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-clock"/></svg></span><div><h4>The research reached the market late</h4><p>It confirmed the differentiator was wrong after the product had been scoped around it. Talking to 43 people is cheap compared to building the wrong thing. It was the same team, the same communities, the same effort, just in the wrong order.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-grid"/></svg></span><div><h4>I designed for a marketplace with supply</h4><p>Most of the screens assume listings, reviews, buyer photos and campaign history exist. The empty states cover the blank case but not the sparse one. Twelve models and four reviews is the state that lasts longest, and I didn't design for it.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-lock"/></svg></span><div><h4>Piracy protection was deferred, not abandoned</h4><p>Working out what the softened version looks like — optional licences, protection as a choice rather than a constraint — was left open when the design work ended.</p></div></div>
</div>
