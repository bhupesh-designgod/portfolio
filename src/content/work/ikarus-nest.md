## Overview

Ikarus Nest was a marketplace and crowdfunding platform for 3D-printable
files. Two kinds of people use it. 3D artists sculpt models and sell them,
either one at a time or as funded campaigns with reward tiers. Hobbyists with
3D printers browse, buy, and print at home.

Ikarus 3D was a studio making production 3D models and AR/VR assets for
brands. Nest was its move into a consumer product, and it was built around one
big idea: stop piracy by never letting the file leave the platform.

I joined the design team to work on the marketplace and creator screens. In a
team of three designers, I owned how the marketplace was organised, its core
screens, the empty and edge states, and a set of reusable components for the
design system. The research that changed the product's direction was the
team's work, and this page says **we** when describing it. The design work
after it is where I say **I**.

The result was a fully specified marketplace, campaign builder, statistics
and design system, handed off to development with behaviour written down
screen by screen.

## The problem

A 3D model is a file. Once a buyer downloads it, the artist has no control over
what happens next. Copies spread. Licences exist, but nothing enforces them
after the download.

<p class="k-hook">The big idea was piracy protection. <em>Remove the download, remove the problem.</em></p>

So Nest was designed around removing the download. Artists could set licences
capping how many times a buyer could print. Files under those licences would
never leave the platform. Buyers would prepare them for printing through a
built-in cloud slicer (the software that turns a model into printer
instructions), and the output would go straight to the printer over wifi.

<p class="k-claim">That was the bet the product was built on. Then we went and
talked to the market.</p>

<div class="k-problem k-wide">
  <div><dt>Who</dt><dd>3D artists who sell printable models for a living, and the hobbyists who buy and print them at home.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Need</dt><dd>Artists need to reach buyers and get paid reliably. Buyers need to find the right file and trust it will print on their machine.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Because</dt><dd>Existing platforms have poor search, slow payouts and thin analytics, and buyers routinely spend money on files that turn out not to print. Piracy is real but, as we were about to learn, already priced in.</dd></div>
  <p class="k-problem__hmw">How might we build a marketplace that both sides trust on day one, when there is no history yet to trust?</p>
</div>

## What we learned from 43 people

<div class="k-gain k-wide">
  <div><b>Goal</b><p>Check the bet with the people it was made for, before more of the product was built around it.</p></div>
  <div><b>What we did</b><p>43 interviews, most over Discord DM, some through Facebook and Instagram, with 27 artists and 16 printer owners. The team's standing in Reddit and Discord communities made this possible; these are not people who answer cold outreach.</p></div>
  <div><b>What we found</b><p>Every artist raised piracy before we asked, and almost none wanted it solved. Buyers wanted to own the file, were loyal to their own slicer, and mostly had printers with no wifi.</p></div>
  <div><b>What it changed</b><p>Piracy protection moved off the critical path and down the roadmap. The first release refocused on campaigns, marketplace, portfolios and community, with reach for artists as the headline.</p></div>
</div>

The artists were not hobbyists. Several had raised between $250,000 and
$680,000 across Kickstarter and MyMiniFactory campaigns. When they talked
about piracy, they were talking about their actual income.

### What the artists said

<p class="k-claim">Every artist raised piracy before we asked. Almost none of
them wanted it solved.</p>

### What the buyers said

<div class="k-why k-wide">
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-download"/></svg></span><div><h4>They want to own the file</h4><p>Several described running a model through two or three tools before it ever reaches a slicer: repairing the mesh, cutting it into printable parts. A file you cannot take out is a file they cannot prepare.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-slice"/></svg></span><div><h4>They are loyal to their slicer</h4><p>Lychee, Chitubox, Cura, Bambu Studio, printer-specific software. One said flatly that everyone likes their own slicer and that locking people in would cut out most of the audience.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-wifi"/></svg></span><div><h4>Most of their printers had no wifi</h4><p>The direct-to-printer model assumed hardware the market did not have.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-clock"/></svg></span><div><h4>Sliced files go stale</h4><p>One artist made a technical point nobody had raised internally. Printer instructions cannot be rescaled, and they date as slicing software improves. Distributing them instead of the source file puts an expiry date on someone's library.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-box"/></svg></span><div><h4>The files are large</h4><p>Between 30 and 300MB. Browsers break on them.</p></div></div>
</div>

<div class="k-band k-wide">

  <div><h4>One feature, three failures.</h4><p>It protected artists from a problem they had priced in, <em>by taking away what buyers considered ownership,</em> using hardware most of them didn't have.</p></div>
</div>

## The turn

<p class="k-claim k-claim--goal">The research did not adjust the product.<br><em>It changed what the product was for.</em></p>

What it left behind was a clear brief. Artists had told us exactly what they
wanted and none of it was piracy protection. They wanted reach, payouts,
analytics, distribution, and a campaign builder that didn't fight them. Buyers
wanted to find the right file and trust it before paying.

## Onboarding

<div class="k-gain k-wide">
  <div><b>Goal</b><p>Get both kinds of user through the door without either one bouncing.</p></div>
  <div><b>What I found in the research</b><p>Two findings pulling in opposite directions. Artists hated verification friction: one said being asked for government ID to run a campaign would put him off; another named identity and payment checks as the reason Kickstarter was the harder platform. Buyers ranked feeling secure enough to hand over card details in their top requirements.</p></div>
  <div><b>What I designed</b><p>A short sign-up (account, email, username, terms) with becoming a creator as a separate, later step. And live campaigns on the sign-up screen itself, as evidence the place is alive.</p></div>
  <div><b>What it changed</b><p>Nobody proves identity until they are about to list something, and the first screen any user sees is already doing the marketplace's job.</p></div>
</div>

## Edge cases

One artist told us that if pin-ups and tasteful nude figures weren't

with the images themselves held behind a warning for signed-out users and a

## Design system and handoff

This was before Figma Dev Mode, so behaviour had to be written down. The
annotations in the file specify conditional logic and interaction, not
labels.

<figure class="k-cap k-wide">
  <div class="k-plate">
    <img src="/assets/projects/nest/annotations.webp" alt="A model detail screen covered in black annotation callouts describing conditional behaviour: which icon appears on GLB files, what happens on clicking like, what a container accepts, and which control expands a section" loading="lazy" width="1601" height="1000" />
  </div>
  <figcaption>The handoff file. Every callout is a rule a developer would otherwise have to invent: which icon appears only on 3D-viewer files, what the like button does to its own state, what a comment box accepts, which control expands the section below it.</figcaption>
</figure>

<ol class="k-notes k-wide">
  <li><span><b>Tooltip on the inactive Publish button</b><span>Appears on hover, explaining what is still missing.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>Minimum three tags, maximum five</b><span>Enforced in the field, not on submit.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>The continue button activates only after the checkbox is ticked</b><span>Stated on the screen, so nobody ships it the other way round.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>This icon appears only on GLB files</b><span>A condition, not a decoration.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>Clicking here expands the section; content slides left on click</b><span>Direction and trigger, both written down.</span></span></li>
  <li><span><b>Asterisked labels mark mandatory fields</b><span>One rule, applied across every form in the file.</span></span></li>
</ol>

## What I learned

<div class="k-alif k-wide">
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-check"/></svg></span><b>What I achieved</b><p>A marketplace, a campaign builder, statistics and a component system where every screen can be traced back to something a real artist or buyer told us. Handed off with the behaviour written down, not left to be guessed.</p></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-clock"/></svg></span><b>The biggest lesson</b><p>The research reached the market late. It confirmed the big idea was wrong after the product had been scoped around it. Talking to 43 people is cheap compared to building the wrong thing. Same team, same communities, same effort, just in the wrong order.</p></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-grid"/></svg></span><b>What I'd do differently</b><p>Design for a sparse marketplace, not just an empty one. Most of my screens assume listings, reviews, buyer photos and campaign history exist. The empty states cover the blank case. Twelve models and four reviews is the state that lasts longest, and I didn't design for it.</p></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-lock"/></svg></span><b>Going forward</b><p>Piracy protection was deferred, not abandoned. Working out what the softened version looks like, protection as a choice rather than a constraint, was left open when the design work ended. It is the question I would pick up first.</p></div>
