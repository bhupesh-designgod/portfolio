## Overview

TBS is the online coaching business of a fitness creator with 404k followers
on Instagram. Clients arrive from an Instagram story, pay over WhatsApp, and
get coached there by voice note. It works, and it stops working at about
thirty clients, because thirty is the point at which one trainer's day and
memory are both full.

I was the product designer who turned that practice into a product. It took
three surfaces: a **sign-up funnel** to bring the right clients in, a **client
app** to carry the daily habit, and a **trainer dashboard** to turn
conversations into a to-do list. This case study is the dashboard: roughly 50
of the project's 60 screens, and the surface everything else quietly depends
on. Every feature in the client app assumes somebody is on the other side of
it, reading check-ins, writing plans, replying. That somebody was running
thirty people out of his own head.

The result is a working tool for one person on a weekday, now in development,
that opens on *who needs me today* instead of *who messaged last*.

## The problem

Before the dashboard, the trainer opened WhatsApp and worked on whoever had
messaged most recently.

<p class="k-hook">Recency set the order of his day. <em>Silence has no notification.</em></p>

That is the whole problem in one sentence. The client who messaged four times
got attention. The client who went quiet three weeks ago, the one actually at
risk of quitting, did not appear anywhere.

He was good at this. That is why it scaled to thirty and not to zero. But the
skill being used was memory, and memory is the one resource you cannot buy
more of. From his side it showed up as four separate things going wrong:

<div class="k-why k-wide">
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-bulb"/></svg></span><div><h4>Memory as infrastructure</h4><p>Thirty cases held in his head: goals, history, injuries, what was tried last month. Retrieved the moment someone messaged.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-bell"/></svg></span><div><h4>No way to prioritise</h4><p>Recency stood in for priority. The loudest client was served first; the disengaged one was invisible.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-doc"/></svg></span><div><h4>Every plan from scratch</h4><p>No templates, no reuse. Writing a plan was a fresh act of memory each time, and the single most expensive thing in his week.</p></div></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-clock"/></svg></span><div><h4>No shape to the day</h4><p>Calls landed whenever a client chose to dial, working hours or not.</p></div></div>
</div>

<div class="k-problem k-wide">
  <div><dt>Who</dt><dd>One online fitness coach with thirty paying clients and the demand to take on many more.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Need</dt><dd>A way to know, each morning, which clients need something from him today, and to write plans without starting from a blank page.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Because</dt><dd>Everything he knows about every client lives in his memory and in a WhatsApp thread. That caps him at thirty, and it hides the clients who have gone quiet, who are the ones most likely to leave.</dd></div>
  <p class="k-problem__hmw">How might we give one coach a workspace that does the remembering and the sorting, so his time goes on coaching?</p>
</div>

<p class="k-claim k-claim--sub">The ceiling was never demand. It was one
person's working memory.</p>

## The goal

<p class="k-claim k-claim--goal">Replace memory<br><em>with a queue.</em></p>

The dashboard answers one question the moment it opens: **who needs me today.**

Not *who are my clients*. A roster only moves the problem onto a screen. It
puts thirty names in front of him and leaves the judgement exactly where it
was. A queue does the judgement.

## How the dashboard is organised

<div class="k-gain k-wide">
  <div><b>Goal</b><p>Decide what the coach sees first, and where everything else lives.</p></div>
  <div><b>What I did</b><p>Mapped the trainer's real week (the things he does every morning, every Sunday, once per client) and gave each recurring job one place to live. Five destinations, no more.</p></div>
  <div><b>What I found</b><p>The landing view had to be the queue, not the client list. Entering through a list means choosing who to work on, and choosing is the work the tool is supposed to remove.</p></div>
  <div><b>What it changed</b><p>Dashboard leads. Clients exists for lookup, one tap away, and even there the default sort is "needs attention", not alphabetical.</p></div>
</div>

## The queue

The landing view sorts clients by what is waiting on them. Not by name, and
not by who messaged last.

Four things can put a client in the queue: **plan pending**, **check-in
submitted**, **call unscheduled**, and **client gone quiet**.

The last one matters most. A client who stops logging and stops replying
creates no notification anywhere. In WhatsApp, their silence looks identical
to contentment. Making absence a queue item is the difference between a tool
that surfaces work and a tool that surfaces noise.

The detailed adherence picture lives on this side on purpose. In the client's
own app a missed target recalculates rather than failing the day, so nobody is
handed a verdict at 1pm. The full pattern (how often, on which days, by how
much) shows up here instead, where a human reads it and decides whether it
matters.

## The plan builder

<div class="k-gain k-wide">
  <div><b>Goal</b><p>Take the most expensive hour of the coach's week, writing a plan, and make it repeatable.</p></div>
  <div><b>What I did</b><p>Broke plan-writing into the order he actually reasons in, then built each step as a screen that narrows the next.</p></div>
  <div><b>What I found</b><p>A single big form would have reproduced the blank page with boxes around it. A sequence carries the reasoning: the calorie base sets the ceiling the meal plan works inside, and review comes before publish because a plan going out wrong costs a week.</p></div>
  <div><b>What it changed</b><p>Six steps, always visible, with the client's details already filled in at step one. This is the decision that actually moves the ceiling.</p></div>
</div>

## Design language

<p class="k-hook">Same system. <em>Opposite theme.</em></p>

Same tokens, same components, same type scale as the client app, rendered
light, because the two products are used in different rooms.

## Decisions and trade-offs

<div class="k-decision k-wide">
  <div class="k-decision__h"><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-list"/></svg></span><h4>Queue over roster</h4></div>
  <dl>
    <div><dt>Decision</dt><dd>The landing view sorts by what is pending, not by client name.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Because</dt><dd>A list moves thirty names out of his head and leaves the judgement where it was.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Trade-off</dt><dd>He cannot scan his whole client book from the landing screen. Clients exists for that, one tap away.</dd></div>
  </dl>
</div>

<div class="k-decision k-wide">
  <div class="k-decision__h"><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-wand"/></svg></span><h4>Steps over a form</h4></div>
  <dl>
    <div><dt>Decision</dt><dd>Plan creation is a six-step sequence, not a single editable page.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Because</dt><dd>Plan-writing was the memory tax. A sequence carries reasoning forward; a form reproduces the blank page.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Trade-off</dt><dd>Slower for the expert case where he already knows every value. Accepted: the expert case is not where the ceiling lives.</dd></div>
  </dl>
</div>

<div class="k-decision k-wide">
  <div class="k-decision__h"><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-chart"/></svg></span><h4>No analytics section</h4></div>
  <dl>
    <div><dt>Decision</dt><dd>No charts layer, no engagement reporting.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Because</dt><dd>There is one trainer and thirty clients. Analytics answers questions nobody has yet, and every screen it adds competes with the queue.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Trade-off</dt><dd>Nothing to show a stakeholder at a glance. There is no stakeholder. That is the point.</dd></div>
  </dl>
</div>

<div class="k-decision k-decision--cut k-wide">
  <div class="k-decision__h"><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-shield"/></svg></span><h4>Blood work comes out</h4><span class="k-tag">Cut</span></div>
  <dl>
    <div><dt>Decision</dt><dd>Removed from both surfaces. Not postponed.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Because</dt><dd>Storing clinical health reports puts the product under health-data regulation, and in European markets that means certification before launch. A large compliance cost attached to a feature nobody signed up for.</dd></div>
    <div><dt>Trade-off</dt><dd>One workflow stays outside the product, which is exactly what this project set out to stop. Worth it: the alternative was a certification programme blocking the launch.</dd></div>
  </dl>
</div>

## What I learned

<div class="k-alif k-wide">
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-check"/></svg></span><b>What I achieved</b><p>A dashboard that opens on the right question and went into development as designed, with the queue, the three-view record and the plan builder all intact.</p></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-bulb"/></svg></span><b>The biggest lesson</b><p>Two finished directions did not ship, and both failed the same way. The feed dashboard and the progress table were complete, and each looked more impressive than what replaced it. The feed showed more; the table showed everything. Both handed the judgement straight back to the person the tool was meant to do the judging for. Showing less on purpose took more discipline than any layout work.</p></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-users"/></svg></span><b>What I'd do differently</b><p>Design for the second trainer earlier. There was exactly one user and he was reachable, so every question had an answer within a day and the queue's sort order is his real logic rather than a guess. But a tool shaped this precisely around one person's habits has assumptions baked in that nobody noticed making, and the second trainer is the one who will find them.</p></div>
  <div><span class="k-tile"><svg class="k-ic"><use href="#i-target"/></svg></span><b>Going forward</b><p>The habit I am keeping is to ask of every screen: does this do the judging, or hand it back? It is a cheap question and it killed two expensive layouts.</p></div>
</div>

<div class="k-close k-wide">
  <p class="k-close__label">In short</p>
  <p>This project delivered the coach's side of TBS: a ~50-screen dashboard built around a queue instead of a client list, a client record split into the three jobs he actually opens it for, and a plan builder that turns an act of memory into a repeatable procedure. It set out to remove the ceiling of one person's working memory, and it did that by letting the tool do the remembering and the sorting while the coaching stayed his. I owned every screen of it.</p>
</div>
